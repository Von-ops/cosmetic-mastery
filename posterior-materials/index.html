Study modeAnswer each prompt out loud or on paper before reading on. After your first honest attempt, skim the relevant section for a hint and try again; check yourself only after two attempts.
  1. What makes a cusp need coverage: the name of the cavity, or the amount/quality of remaining tooth?
  2. Would you remove a healthy cusp just to make a preparation more symmetrical?
  3. If you cannot keep the margin clean and dry, can a stronger ceramic rescue the bond?

Write an answer, then a second attempt after a hint. Teach-back: explain your reasoning in ordinary language, not product names.

1 A vocabulary anchored to the tooth

An inlay fits within the cusps without replacing a cusp. An onlay replaces/covers one or more cusps. An overlay usually covers the whole occlusal table/all cusps. Terminology varies; draw the planned coverage on the lab prescription. An occlusal veneer/table top is a thin occlusal partial-coverage form; it is not automatically indicated for every worn tooth. Coverage describes anatomy, not a material.

A large MOD with thin undermined cusps is not made safe by placing a prettier intracoronal inlay. Conversely, a small lesion with sound cusps may be treated directly; indirect treatment is not an achievement to pursue for its own sake. Compare direct composite, bonded partial coverage, full crown, and monitoring when appropriate.

2 Diagnose the tooth and patient

Assess symptoms, pulp status, periapical condition, caries activity, cracks, existing restorations, contact/occlusion, periodontal condition, parafunction and ability to isolate. Use transillumination/magnification and appropriate radiographs; no single test excludes a crack. A cracked tooth needs pulpal/periodontal prognosis, not a mechanical “onlay cures all cracks” assumption. Document remaining wall support after removing failing material, and reconsider the plan when the actual defect is visible.

First cases should have controlled disease, a manageable supragingival margin, straightforward contacts, room for the prescribed material and no untreated occlusal disorder. Refer/mentor complex crack, pulpal, deep-margin or extensive wear cases. Endodontic status alone does not dictate one restoration for every tooth.

3 Match material to optical and mechanical needs

IPS e.max Press is a practical lab-first candidate: etchable lithium-disilicate glass ceramic with indicated partial coverage and veneers. The lab can press a wax/digital design and finish monolithically or with specified cut-back/layering. IPS e.max CAD is also lithium disilicate but milled and crystallized; a lab can mill it. “CAD” does not mean the dentist must own a mill.

IPS Empress CAD is leucite glass ceramic, not lithium disilicate. It can serve suitable single-unit esthetic indications but is not the default answer for every high-load posterior defect. Both are etchable glass ceramics, but the specified HF times differ. IPS Empress Direct is a resin composite, despite sharing “Empress” in its name. Material identity must be written in full.

Zirconia is an oxide ceramic: different bonding chemistry, not HF-etchable. It is not an automatic upgrade for a conservative bonded inlay. A more fracture-resistant bulk material does not guarantee a durable bond or tooth prognosis. Flexural-strength marketing numbers are not clinical rankings.

4 Translucency is a design decision

The final appearance depends on stump shade, ceramic thickness/translucency, lab characterization and cement shade. More translucent material may blend but reveal a dark substrate. More opaque material may mask but limits light reaching a light-cure cement. Photograph the hydrated shade before isolation. Communicate stump shade after preparation and desired final contour. Cement should fine-tune a planned optical solution, not rescue the wrong ceramic after fabrication.

5 Lab workflow, with only a milling sidebar

Records → approved design → preparation/scan or impression → temporary → lab fabrication → try-in → definitive adhesive delivery. In the prescription specify tooth, coverage drawing, material, shade/translucency, stump shade, contacts, occlusion, technique, minimum thickness checks and whether the intaglio has already been etched/primed. Require the lab to report pretreatment. Same-day milling compresses logistics but does not eliminate diagnostics, crystallization, isolation or bonding.

⚑ Cases, pearls & pitfalls

A: Small MO lesion, supported cusps, easy isolation. Defensible first option: direct composite, if clinical findings support it—not automatic ceramic.
B: Broad old MOD, cracked/unsupported distolingual cusp, adequate isolation. Consider selective cusp coverage after diagnosis; “onlay” means cover the affected cusp, not blindly flatten all cusps.
C: Deep bleeding margin and a dark endodontically treated tooth. Stop to establish periodontal/pulpal/restorative prognosis and isolation feasibility before ordering an opaque block.
Pearls
  • diagnose before prescribing
  • use exact product name
  • preserve useful enamel
  • have the lab confirm surface treatment
Pitfalls
  • e.max means zirconia
  • every MOD means crown
  • stronger ceramic compensates for saliva
  • veneer cement shades solve all masking problems
